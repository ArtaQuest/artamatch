Can two birth dates predict whether a marriage lasts thirty years?

An astrology-only model, trained on couples born 1600–1850 and tested on couples born 1851–1900 — a split by time, into a window where everybody is certainly dead, so no marriage is still running and none was cut short by the records ending. Reported against the one baseline it is allowed: a two-parameter logistic on the signed subtraction of the two dates.

01Score a pair

Two dates in, one probability out. Every planetary position is computed here, in your browser, when you press the button — there is no lookup table of answers and no cache.

Leave the month or day on — when it is not known: a third of the training data is incomplete the same way, and the model is told the precision rather than left to guess it.

02Search the dates

Holds one birth date fixed and scores every candidate date in a range, then ranks them. There is no city dimension: the model's only inputs are two dates.

03What each tradition contributes

Every tradition that can be computed from two dates is in the stack, with up to three feature blocks each — a per-tradition guarantee rather than a global cut, so no tradition is starved by a stronger neighbour. The AUC is that tradition's own blocks, scored alone, on the held-out couples. Each row opens to say what the tradition actually computes and which blocks it contributes.

04The precision grid — how much a vague date costs

Real birth data arrives to the day, to the month, to the year, or not at all. So each partner's date is degraded independently and the model is scored in every combination, on the same couples each time — the held-out fifth, split by person group so nobody in it appears in training. 05What this is, and what it is not

A score here is a model output, not a fact about anybody. The label is whether Wikidata's record of a marriage spans thirty years or more — which is a fact about what was written down, not about what happened. A marriage whose ending was never recorded is dated from the earlier spouse's death instead. Nothing on this page is advice.
  • Astrology only. Every feature comes from a tradition module: charts, aspects, harmonics, divisional charts, calendars, heliacal risings. No cohort, exposure, nationality or sex variable is a feature.
  • One baseline, and it is reported every time. A two-parameter logistic on dob_woman − dob_man in years, signed. Positive means the man is older. The signed gap is not a nuisance to be removed; it is the number astrology has to beat.
  • The browser runs the training code. Not a port of it — the same core.py and the same trad_*.py modules, with Swiss Ephemeris replaced by a pure-numpy shim because Pyodide has no pyswisseph. The shim is verified against the real library — longitudes to 0.005°, the same as the ephemeris' own storage step, all 21 ayanamsas to 0.003°, eclipse dates and calendar conversion exact — and a whole build of every feature block agrees with a chunked one bit for bit.
  • Nothing is cached. No precomputed scores, no candidate grid, no memo between calls. The one table that ships is the ephemeris itself — positions of planets, which is what Swiss Ephemeris' own data files are, not answers about people.
  • Two dates is the whole input. Birthplaces were dropped, so there is no Ascendant, no house cusp and no astrocartography line, and every chart is cast at 08:00 UT.
  • Person-disjoint validation, five folds of 80/20. A person appears in more than one partnership, so folds are split by person group, never by row. Every number in the grid is out-of-fold, and a degraded row is scored by the fold-model that never saw it.
  • The split is the experiment, and it is a split in TIME. Couples born up to 1850 train; couples born 1851–1900 are held out. So the question is not “rank couples drawn from the years you learned from” but “learn from the earlier couples and predict the later ones”, which is the harder and the honest one. An earlier version of this project split at random and scored 0.6452 within its training era against 0.5159 out of it — a gap of 0.13 that was entirely the model interpolating a calendar it had already seen.
  • Nothing here is censored, which is why the window ends at 1900. Everybody born on or before 1900 is dead. That matters more for this question than for any other: a marriage still running cannot be labelled, and one that has not yet had thirty years cannot reach thirty. Every marriage in this dataset has ended and every positive was observable — unlike the parenthood question this project asked before, where a couple born in 1990 simply had not finished.
  • The base rate still moves across the boundary, and it moves the other way. About 32% of the training marriages reach thirty years against 44% of the held-out ones: earlier-born couples died younger and their records are thinner. Read the per-tradition table below against the era rule reported beside it, not against chance.
  • The sharpest confound is which way the marriage ended. Marriages with a recorded end date reach thirty years about 16% of the time; marriages that ran until a death do so about 53% of the time. That is not an input — a model cannot see which case a couple is in — but it correlates with things that are, so a case-only reference is reported alongside every score.
  • A date can say “I do not know”. A third of the training dates are incomplete, and an unknown component is written 00 — 1889-00-00 is a year, 1889-04-00 a month. Wikidata pads an unknown day with 01 instead, which would make a year-only birth indistinguishable from a genuine 1 January birthday and let the model read date precision as a proxy for how well documented somebody is. The controls above accept the same encoding, and the model is told the precision rather than left to infer it.

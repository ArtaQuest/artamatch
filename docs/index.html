Every marriage algorithm we could find, measured the honest part

Divorce is an outcome, not a verdict: a quiet parting is not a bad marriage, and lasting until death is not automatically a good one. So the target is a second dataset — 10,000 marriages judged one at a time from what the record actually says, read across 21 languages, each verdict carrying its evidence and its sources (download, method).

The clearest thing in it has nothing to do with the stars. Against a base rate of 59% good, couples who built something together come out at 89% (a business) and 82% (a body of work); couples who only had children come out at 75%. Making something together beats procreation alone.

We went looking for every compatibility system that answers the marriage question directly — not astrology applied to a couple, but the traditions whose own literature is about whether two people should marry. Eighteen were added: the Javanese weton calculation, which by headcount is the most-used marriage algorithm on earth; the Balinese pawukon, with ten week-cycles of different lengths running at once; the ten porutham of Tamil and Sinhala practice, which is not Ashtakoota; Papasamya, which asks not who is afflicted but whether the two are afflicted equally; the two Chinese relations the bank was missing (Xiang Xing and Xiang Po); Korean gunghap in its outer and inner readings; Burmese Mahabote; the couple's I Ching hexagram, where his number makes the upper trigram and hers the lower, so the hexagram exists only for the pair; the geomantic Judge of ilm al-raml, which is literally two figures added line by line; biorhythm, whose only published use is compatibility; the Hellenistic degree techniques (Sabian symbols, dodekatemoria, monomoiria); the Kabbalistic 72 Names wheel and the Sefer Yetzirah letters; the Norse runic half-months; the Egyptian Nile zodiac; and the Aztec tonalpohualli with its thirteen day lords. Each is anchored against a dated event — Indonesian independence is Jemuwah Legi, every Galungan is Buda Kliwon Dungulan, the fall of Tenochtitlan is 1 Coatl — and the anchors are asserted every time the code runs.

Some famous systems cannot be here, and it is fairer to say so than to fake them. Human Design, the Vertex, Juno and all house-based synastry need a birth time; astrocartography needs a place; Ifá and tarot spreads need a casting, not a date. Stable-matching algorithms and the Gottman ratio are not functions of a birth date at all. This page has two dates and nothing else.

Then each system was fitted on its own, cross-validated on the training couples, never touching the held-out set — because a joint model cannot answer "does Ashtakoota work", only "what does the Lasso keep". Of the 44 named systems measured, 8 score above the age-gap baseline of 0.5122: Rudhyar cycle phases (0.5601), Western synastry aspects (0.5521), Maya Tzolkin and the oracle (0.5164), Ashtakoota / Guna Milan (0.5160), Tarot birth cards (0.5153), Nadi and Bhakoot (0.5142), Ten Porutham (0.5131), Kabbalah: 72 Names (0.5130). But a cross-validated AUC on 7,909 couples carries a standard error near 0.0066, so only 2 of them clear it by more than that — Rudhyar cycle phases at 0.5601 and Western synastry aspects at 0.5521. The other six sit inside the noise. Everything below them — the weton, the pawukon, the porutham, the hexagram, the Judge, biorhythm, all of it — lands at chance. That is the result, and we are publishing it rather than burying it.

Every statement reads BOTH dates, and the test for that had to be rebuilt three times. A statement was once called pair-only if its NAME lacked a "his" or "her" — a test of spelling, not of behaviour. The behavioural test builds synthetic couples on a grid of fixed midpoints and widening separations: hold the midpoint, move the two births apart, and a real interaction changes while an era quantity does not. Its first three versions were each wrong in a way that only showed up when we looked: the separations were all whole years, so every tradition keyed to the tropical calendar kept the same day of the year at every step and was condemned for standing still; the score counted groups in which the value moved, which rises mechanically as the grid grows, so a threshold stopped meaning the same thing from one run to the next; and the sample was small enough that rare statements never fired at all and were recorded as era quantities when the truth was that we had no evidence about them. The test now decomposes each statement's variance into the part explained by the separation and the part explained by the midpoint, over 7,350 synthetic couples, and abstains out loud where it cannot see. A statement ships only if more than half its variance is about the pair (every score).

Under that gate the bank is 1,214 statements and the model keeps 110 of them: cross-validated 0.5612, and 0.5276 on couples it never saw — +1.75 standard errors above chance and +2.68 above the age-gap baseline of 0.4853, the one comparator we measure against because it reads nothing but the same two dates. Its weight is spread across the sky you were both born under (28%) · how you meet day to day (25%) · how your feelings meet theirs (19%) · the contacts you do not choose (15%) · what holds you and what opens you (13%). It can express 7,793 distinct scores, so it genuinely orders candidate dates rather than sorting them into a handful of ties.

The gate is where the score went, and the trade is worth stating plainly. Loosening it buys cross-validation almost monotonically — at a tenth of the variance the same machinery reaches 0.5735, and with no gate at all the old published figure was 0.5872. Those numbers are higher because they are partly reading when you were born rather than who you are. Tightening it past a half destroys the model completely: at nine tenths, nothing survives selection at any penalty. We hold the gate at a half on principle and not on score.

Three cautions. The cross-validated figure 0.5612 and the held-out 0.5276 differ by more than the seed spread, which is what a 110-statement model does on 7,909 couples — trust the smaller one. The held-out set was read several times while the gate was being rebuilt; every choice between candidates was made on cross-validation or on doctrine, never on those reads, but the set is no longer as fresh as a single-read protocol would leave it, and that is a cost we are naming rather than hiding. And the AUC measures ranking ACROSS couples, while the product ranks dates WITHIN one person's window. Those are different questions and only the first is measured. Read it as a curiosity, not a forecast.

The browser runs the same code, and we checked rather than assumed. Scoring 300 held-out couples through the page's own path and comparing all 110 statements one by one gives 1 disagreement in 33,000 — a single aspect sitting 16 arcseconds from its orb edge, inside the browser ephemeris's own precision. Finding it took fixing a real fault: four slow-planet pairs were missing from the page's cycle list, so three shipped statements were silently never firing in the browser while the model still carried a weight for them.

How it works 30 seconds

Each tradition makes small, checkable statements — “her Moon is in Rohini”, “his Saturn squares her Moon”. We wrote down thousands of them, and five centuries of recorded marriages decided which few matter: a sparse regression kept 495, every one reading both birth dates. A match's score is simply the handful of surviving rules it stirs — the fewer, the better. The percentage shown is how often matches in that band held for life.
